🛒 6.5 Newton's Second Law of Motion

F = m·a — the master key. More force → more acceleration; more mass → less acceleration.

F = m·a made easy in Hinglish — plus why airbags and 'pulling your hands back' save you from injury.

🛒Force produces acceleration — how are they related?

A force can start motion, stop it, or change velocity. A change in velocity means the object is accelerating. So a net force produces acceleration. From everyday experience we can guess:

💪 Bigger force

Push a ball gently → small acceleration. Push it hard → large acceleration. For the same object, larger force ⇒ larger acceleration.

🏋️ Bigger mass

With the same force, a light object is easier to set in motion than a heavy one. Larger mass ⇒ smaller acceleration.

🔬 How scientists tested this (Activity 6.3 & 6.4)

A cardboard cart on wheels is pulled by a thread over a pulley with a paper cup holding coins. By doubling the mass in the cup (doubling the force) on the same cart, the time taken halves — the cart's acceleration increased (a = 2s/T²). Then keeping the force same and doubling the cart's mass, the acceleration became about half. Conclusion: a ∝ F (mass fixed) and a ∝ 1/m (force fixed).

NEWTON'S SECOND LAW OF MOTIONWhen a net force acts on an object, the object accelerates in the direction of the net force. magnitude of acceleration ∝ net force   and   a ∝ 1 / mass
F = m · a   ⇔   a = F ÷ m

The direction of acceleration is always the same as the direction of the net force. Mass in kg, acceleration in m/s², force in N.

📏One newton defined — and the force of gravity

📐
One newton is defined as the force that produces an acceleration of 1 m/s² on an object of mass 1 kg.
1 N = 1 kg × 1 m/s² = 1 kg m/s²

How much is 1 N really? If you hold a 100 g mass (about two 50-₹ or 2×50 g packets) in your palm, the upward force your palm applies is roughly 1 N.

🌍 Weight — the gravitational force (F = mg)

When an object falls freely towards the Earth, the acceleration involved is the acceleration due to gravitational force, denoted g ≈ 9.8 m/s² (≈ 10 m/s² for quick calculations). Using F = ma, the gravitational force (weight) on a mass m is:

WEIGHTF (weight) = m · g   •   g = 9.8 m/s² ≈ 10 m/s²
💡
Note: g does not depend on the mass of the falling object — all objects near the Earth's surface fall with the same acceleration (if air resistance is ignored).

⏱️Momentum form & impulse — why “pulling your hands back” saves you

Newton's second law is more completely stated using momentum. Momentum = mass × velocity. The second law says: the rate of change of momentum of an object is equal to the net force acting on it. From this we get the famous impulse idea:

IMPULSE = CHANGE IN MOMENTUMF × t = m(v − u)   ⇒   F = m(v − u) ÷ t

For a fixed change in velocity, increasing the time t reduces the force F. This single idea protects us every day:

🏏 Catching a cricket ball

A fielder pulls their hands backwards with the ball — the time to stop the ball increases, so the force on the hands is smaller and there is less injury.

🎈 Airbags & seat belts

In a crash the airbag inflates and increases the stopping time of your head/chest → smaller force on your body.

🥥 Breaking a coconut

The coconut hits the hard ground and stops in a very short time → a very large force breaks the shell.

🛋️ Landing mats & sand

High-jump mats and sand pits let athletes sink in, increasing stopping time and reducing the force (and injury).

✏️Worked examples from the chapter

Example 6.4: A weightlifter holds a barbell of total mass 30 kg (bar 10 kg + 10 kg on each side) steady. How much force does she apply?
Gravitational force downward, F = mg = 30 × 9.8 = 294 N. To hold it steady (no acceleration), she must push upwards with an equal force of 294 N.
Example 6.5: A student pushes a stationary 25 kg block on a horizontal floor. Maximum friction is 50 N. Find the displacement in 2 s if the student pushes with (i) 50 N and (ii) 55 N forward.
(i) F = 50 N: applied force equals friction → net force = 0 → the block remains stationary, displacement = 0.
(ii) F = 55 N: net force = 55 − 50 = 5 N. Acceleration a = F/m = 5 ÷ 25 = 0.2 m/s². From s = ut + ½at² = 0 + ½ × 0.2 × (2)² = 0.4 m forward in 2 seconds.
Example 6.6: A 1500 kg sports car moves east. Its v–t graph shows: 0–5 s it speeds up from 0 to 10 m/s; 5–10 s speed stays 10 m/s; 10–15 s it slows to 0. Find the force in each interval.
(i) 0–5 s: a = (10−0)/5 = 2 m/s² → F = 1500 × 2 = 3000 N east.
(ii) 5–10 s: velocity constant → a = 0 → F = 0.
(iii) 10–15 s: a = (0−10)/5 = −2 m/s² → F = 1500 × (−2) = −3000 N (i.e. 3000 N west, opposite to motion).

?State Newton's second law of motion.🎧▾

When a net force acts on an object, the object accelerates in the direction of the net force. The magnitude of the acceleration is proportional to the magnitude of the net force and inversely proportional to the mass of the object. Direction of acceleration = direction of net force.

Constant velocity means acceleration = 0. From the first/second law, net force = mass × acceleration = 0. So the net force on the toy car is zero (even though it is moving).

📗Book Questions & Answers on this topic

Pause-and-Ponder, Short and Long answers for §6.5.

1Pause and Ponder: A toy car of mass 100 g is moving with a constant velocity of 0.5 m/s. What is the net force acting on it?NCERT · P&P Q6▾ Answer
Answer —

The car moves with constant velocity ⇒ acceleration = 0 ⇒ net force = 0 (from F = ma = m × 0).

2Pause and Ponder: Two children of different masses sit on identical swings. To give the same initial acceleration, for which child would you need a larger force?NCERT · P&P Q7▾ Answer
Answer —

The heavier child. F = m·a — for the same acceleration, the force needed is directly proportional to mass, so the larger-mass child needs the larger force.

3Pause and Ponder: How are glass items packed with bubble wrap or hay protected from damage?NCERT · P&P Q8▾ Answer
Answer —

The soft packing increases the time over which the glass items slow down or stop during jerks. A larger stopping time means a smaller force of impact (F = m·Δv/Δt), so the items do not break.

4Short Answer: State Newton's second law. Write F = ma and define one newton using it.Book · Short Q7▾ Answer
Answer —

Second law: when a net force acts on an object, the object accelerates in the direction of the net force; the acceleration is proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to the mass. Mathematically a = F/m, i.e. F = m·a (F = net force in N, m = mass in kg, a = acceleration in m/s²). One newton is the force that produces an acceleration of 1 m/s² on a mass of 1 kg; 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².

5Short Answer: A student pushes a stationary 20 kg block; maximum friction is 40 N. Find the acceleration when the push is (i) 40 N and (ii) 50 N.Book · Short Q8▾ Answer
Answer —

(i) Push = friction = 40 N → net force = 0 → acceleration = 0 m/s² (block stays stationary).
(ii) Net force = 50 − 40 = 10 N → a = F/m = 10/20 = 0.5 m/s² in the forward direction.

6Short Answer: What is the gravitational force? Write F for an object of mass m and give the value of g.Book · Short Q9▾ Answer
Answer —

The gravitational force is the force with which the Earth attracts every object towards itself. For an object of mass m, F = m·g. Near the Earth's surface g = 9.8 m/s² (≈ 10 m/s² for quick estimates). Unlike force, g does not depend on the object's mass — all objects experience the same gravitational acceleration.

7Short Answer: Explain with F = m·Δv/Δt why a cricketer pulls hands back and why airbags and landing mats reduce injury.Book · Short Q11▾ Answer
Answer —

When catching a fast ball, the change in velocity Δv is fixed. By pulling the hands backward, the time Δt increases. Since F = m·Δv/Δt, a bigger Δt gives a smaller force on the hands. Airbags, landing mats and sand pits work on the same principle — they increase the time of impact so the force experienced is smaller.

8Long Answer: State the second law and derive F = ma. Give a solved example (take g = 9.8 m/s²).Book · Long Q1▾ Answer
Answer —

Second law: when a net force acts on an object, it accelerates in the direction of the net force; acceleration ∝ net force and ∝ 1/mass.

Derivation of F = ma: For fixed mass, experiments show a ∝ F. For a fixed force, a ∝ 1/m. Combining, a ∝ F/m, so a = k·F/m. Choosing SI units such that k = 1 gives F = m·a.

Solved example: A weightlifter holds a barbell of total mass 30 kg steady. Downward gravitational force = mg = 30 × 9.8 = 294 N. To keep it steady (net force 0) she applies an equal upward force of 294 N.

9Long Answer: A 1500 kg sports car moves east. From its v–t graph, velocity rises 0→10 m/s in 0–5 s, stays 10 m/s during 5–10 s, and falls 10→0 m/s during 10–15 s. Find the force in each interval.Book · Long Q3▾ Answer
Answer —

This is the same motion as Example 6.6 solved in the lesson above — here is the exam-style answer:
(i) 0–5 s: a = (10−0)/5 = 2 m/s² → F = 1500 × 2 = 3000 N east.
(ii) 5–10 s: velocity constant → a = 0 → F = 0 N.
(iii) 10–15 s: a = (0−10)/5 = −2 m/s² → F = 1500 × (−2) = −3000 N, i.e. 3000 N towards the west (opposite to motion).

🪙Newton's First Law of Motion

NEWTON'S FIRST LAW OF MOTIONAn object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion keeps moving with a constant velocity — UNLESS a net force acts on it.

In other words: if the net force on an object is zero, the object cannot start moving, and if it is moving it cannot change its velocity. In such a case its acceleration is zero.

  • At rest means velocity = 0.
  • Constant velocity means no change in speed AND no change in direction — motion in a straight line at a fixed speed.
🚌
A body with zero net force is said to be in equilibrium. Remember: “no net force” can mean no forces at all, or many forces that cancel out.

🔭Galileo's thought experiment — the road to the first law

For many centuries people wrongly believed that a force is always needed to keep an object moving. It was Galileo Galilei (17th century) who corrected this with brilliant thought experiments:

⬇️ Ball rolling down

On a frictionless incline going down, speed increases.

⬆️ Ball rolling up

On a frictionless incline going up, speed decreases.

➡️ Ball on horizontal plane

Between these two cases — on a level frictionless surface the speed stays constant forever.

Galileo argued: if a body moves along a horizontal plane and all impediments to its motion are removed (no friction), it will continue to move indefinitely at constant velocity.

👨‍🔬
Meet a scientist: Isaac Newton used the word inertia for the tendency of objects to resist a change in their state of rest or uniform motion, and used Galileo's idea to frame his first law of motion (1687). The unit of force is named after him.

🏋️Inertia — the “laziness” of objects

INERTIAThe natural tendency of an object to RESIST any change in its state of rest or uniform motion

Mass is the measure of inertia. Heavier objects are harder to start moving and harder to stop — they have more inertia. That is why Newton's first law is also called the law of inertia.

Type of inertiaMeaningDaily-life example
Inertia of restBody resists being set into motion.You fall backwards when a bus suddenly starts; a coin drops into a tumbler when the card under it is flicked away; dust flies off a beaten carpet.
Inertia of motionMoving body resists being stopped.You fall forwards when a moving bus suddenly stops; an athlete runs before a long jump; snow flies off shoes struck against a wall.
Inertia of directionBody resists change in its direction of motion.You are thrown outward when a car takes a sharp turn; a stone tied to a string flies off tangentially when the string breaks.
🧠
Force from the first law: force is what changes or tries to change the state of rest or uniform motion of a body. If the applied force is not enough, it only “tries” but produces no visible change.

📈Worked examples — zero net force cases

Example 6.2: A person pushes a moving box forward with a force exactly equal to the friction between the box and the floor. Will the box continue or stop?
Friction acts backward. The two forces (push and friction) are equal and opposite → they balance → net force = 0. By the first law, the box continues moving with constant velocity.
Example 6.3: Draw (i) position-time and (ii) velocity-time graphs when NO net force acts on an object.
Case A — object at rest: position never changes → position-time graph is a horizontal straight line; velocity stays zero → velocity-time graph is a line on the time axis.
Case B — object moving with constant velocity: position increases steadily → position-time graph is a slanted straight line (its slope = velocity); velocity is constant → velocity-time graph is a horizontal straight line above the time axis.

Tip: in a velocity–time graph, a horizontal line means zero acceleration; a slanted line means constant acceleration (net force ≠ 0).

📗Book Questions & Answers on this topic

Pause-and-Ponder and Book questions for §6.4 First Law.

1Pause and Ponder: An object is moving with a constant velocity. Is there a net force acting upon it?NCERT · P&P Q3▾ Answer
Answer —

No. Constant velocity means acceleration = 0. From the first law, a net force is needed only to change velocity. Since velocity is not changing, the net force must be zero.

2Pause and Ponder: No net force acts on an object. Which situations are possible — (i) stays at rest if at rest, (ii) keeps constant velocity if moving, (iii) moves with constant acceleration?NCERT · P&P Q4▾ Answer
Answer —

Possibilities (i) and (ii) are correct: the object continues in its existing state of rest or uniform motion. Situation (iii) is not possible — constant acceleration means velocity is changing, which requires a net force.

3Pause and Ponder: In the real world it is hard to find an object with no forces on it. How can we still make the net force zero? Explain with an example.NCERT · P&P Q5▾ Answer
Answer —

We add extra forces so that all the forces cancel. Example: push a box on the floor with a force exactly equal to the force of friction. The two forces are equal and opposite; the net force becomes zero and the box slides with constant velocity. A book on a table is another example — weight down, normal force up.

4Exercise: For a ball on a smooth frictionless surface, choose the correct option: (i) no net force → velocity remains the same / increases / decreases; (ii) net force along motion → speed remains the same / increases / decreases; (iii) net force opposite to motion → speed remains the same / increases / decreases.NCERT · Exercise Q2▾ Answer
Answer —

(i) With no net force the velocity of the ball will remain the same (first law).
(ii) A net force in the direction of motion makes the ball increase its speed.
(iii) A net force opposite to the motion makes the ball decrease its speed (and eventually reverse it).

5Exercise: The position-time graphs of four objects A, B, C and D are given. On which object(s) does a net force act?NCERT · Exercise Q6▾ Answer
Answer —

A net force acts only on objects whose velocity is changing (acceleration ≠ 0). In a position–time graph: horizontal line ⇒ at rest; straight slanted line ⇒ constant velocity (no net force); curved graph ⇒ velocity changing ⇒ net force acting. Hence a net force acts on Object C and Object D — those whose graphs are curved.

6Short Answer: State Newton's first law of motion. What does it tell us about motion when the net force is zero? Give one example.Book · Short Q5▾ Answer
Answer —

Newton's first law: an object at rest remains at rest and an object in motion continues to move with constant velocity unless a net force acts upon it. If the net force is zero the object cannot start moving and cannot change its velocity — acceleration is zero. Example: a person pushes a box with a force exactly equal to friction; net force is zero, so the box slides at constant velocity.

7Short Answer: What is inertia? Who introduced it? Why is the first law called the law of inertia?Book · Short Q6▾ Answer
Answer —

Inertia is the tendency of an object to resist a change in its state of rest or of uniform motion. Isaac Newton introduced the word (Galileo had earlier used thought experiments about frictionless motion). The first law describes the natural behaviour of objects in the absence of a net force — they continue in their existing state due to inertia — so it is called the law of inertia.

8Long Answer: Describe Galileo's thought experiment that led to the idea of inertia. How did Newton extend it into the first law? Explain with two real-life examples why continuous force is sometimes needed to keep an object moving.Book · Long Q4▾ Answer
Answer —

Galileo's thought experiment: For centuries people believed a force is always needed to keep a body moving. Galileo imagined a ball on inclined planes: rolling down a frictionless slope it speeds up, rolling up it slows, and on a perfectly smooth horizontal plane it would keep moving forever with constant velocity. So no force is needed to maintain uniform motion — a force is needed only to change it.

Newton's extension: Newton called this tendency of bodies inertia and framed the first law: an object at rest stays at rest and an object in motion keeps constant velocity unless a net force acts on it. The law tells us force is needed only to change the state of motion (to cause acceleration).

Why continuous force is sometimes needed in real life: because friction is always present and keeps opposing motion, so we apply a force just to balance friction (keeping net force zero). Examples: (1) cycling — you stop pedalling and the cycle slows and stops due to friction and air resistance, so you keep pedalling to maintain speed; (2) pushing a box — once you stop pushing, friction stops it, so to slide it at steady speed you keep pushing with a force equal to the friction.
